A rocket nozzle is a small, hot, very loud bit of fluid mechanics. Its job is to take high-pressure gas from the combustion chamber and convert as much of that pressure energy into directed kinetic energy as possible. The shape — converging, then diverging, like an hourglass on its side — does the conversion.

## Subsonic in, supersonic out

In a converging duct, **subsonic** gas accelerates as the area shrinks (think: garden-hose nozzle). But at exactly Mach 1, something flips: in a **diverging** duct, supersonic gas accelerates further as the area *grows*. This is one of those facts you have to take on faith from compressible-flow class, then it stops surprising you.

So the converging-diverging (Laval) nozzle is two ideas glued together:

1. Converging section squeezes the chamber gas through the **throat** at exactly Mach 1. This is called **choked flow** — the mass-flow rate through the throat is fixed by chamber conditions, regardless of what's downstream.
2. Diverging section expands the supersonic flow, dropping pressure further and ramping velocity up to whatever the geometry allows.

## The expansion ratio

The single most important nozzle parameter is the **area ratio**:

$$
\epsilon = \frac{A_e}{A_t}
$$

Exit area over throat area. Bigger $\epsilon$ means more expansion, lower exit pressure, faster exhaust. For an ideal gas in choked flow:

$$
\frac{A_e}{A_t} = \left(\frac{2}{k+1}\right)^{\!1/(k-1)} \frac{1}{M_e} \left(1 + \frac{k-1}{2} M_e^2\right)^{\!(k+1)/2(k-1)}
$$

Don't memorize this. The shape that matters: larger $\epsilon$ → higher exit Mach → more thrust. But you can't go arbitrarily large.

## Over- and under-expanded

The pressure thrust term from chapter 2 was $(p_e - p_\infty) A_e$. The nozzle designer picks $\epsilon$ to set $p_e$. Three cases:

- $p_e > p_\infty$ — **under-expanded**. Gas keeps expanding after leaving the nozzle. Some thrust left on the table. Common in vacuum-optimized engines that fire at sea level briefly during testing.
- $p_e = p_\infty$ — **perfectly expanded**. Maximum thrust at this altitude.
- $p_e < p_\infty$ — **over-expanded**. Gas is at lower pressure than the surrounding air. Flow can separate from the nozzle wall, which is structurally bad. Severe over-expansion produces visible Mach diamonds and can shake an engine apart.

Sea-level launchers compromise: small enough $\epsilon$ that the engine doesn't over-expand badly at liftoff, but large enough to still produce decent $I_{sp}$ at altitude. Vacuum-stage engines use much bigger $\epsilon$ — the RL10 sits at ~84:1, the J-2 at ~28:1. Compare to an Estes nozzle at maybe 4:1.

> **Hobby aside.** A black-powder motor's nozzle is a small ceramic insert. You can't tune $\epsilon$ once it's pressed — but you *can* see the consequence: the exhaust plume's shape. A clean, parallel jet means matched expansion; a flared or diamond-pattern plume means it's off-design.

Most of the math here is for understanding, not for picking parts. For model and high-power rocketry, the manufacturer already nailed the nozzle; your job is to pick the right motor for your rocket's mass and impulse needs. The next chapter is where you actually do work as a designer.
