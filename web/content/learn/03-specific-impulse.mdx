Specific impulse, $I_{sp}$, is the single most quoted number in propulsion. It's a fuel-efficiency rating for rocket engines.

The definition is straightforward:

<Eq label="(3.1)">
$$
I_{sp} = \frac{F}{\dot{m}\, g_0}
$$
</Eq>

Force per weight-flow of propellant. Units come out as **seconds**, which is universally confusing on first contact. Here's how to read it: if your engine produces $F$ Newtons of thrust, $g_0 \cdot I_{sp}$ tells you how many seconds you could keep that thrust running if you burned propellant at a rate of 1 N of weight per second.

But the easier mental model: $I_{sp}$ is just $v_e / g_0$ in disguise. A higher $I_{sp}$ means faster exhaust, which means more push per kilogram of propellant. That's it.

## A feel for the numbers

| Engine | Propellant | $I_{sp}$ (vac, s) |
|---|---|---|
| Estes C6 | Black powder | ~80 |
| Estes E12 | Black powder | ~95 |
| AeroTech H128W | APCP composite | ~210 |
| RD-180 | Kerosene / LOX | 338 |
| F-1 (Saturn V S-IC) | Kerosene / LOX | 304 |
| J-2 (Saturn V S-II) | LH2 / LOX | 421 |
| RS-25 (Space Shuttle) | LH2 / LOX | 452 |
| NEXT ion engine | Xenon (electric) | 4170 |

Two things to notice:

1. **Chemical rockets cap out around 450 s** for LH2/LOX. There's no clever chemistry left to wring more out of the energy density. Past that, you switch to electric propulsion (high $I_{sp}$, tiny thrust) or nuclear (great $I_{sp}$, regulatory nightmare).

2. **Hobby motors are an order of magnitude below liquid first stages.** That's why model rockets need such a high mass-fraction of propellant for even short hops — chapter 4 (the rocket equation) will make this brutally concrete.

## The thrust curve

A real motor's thrust isn't constant. Black-powder motors have a sharp peak followed by a slow tail; composite motors look more rectangular. The $I_{sp}$ quoted on the label is an average; the curve is what determines your max acceleration and where the rocket leaves the launch rod.
